Sibling rivalry:
when brothers fall out

An adapted classroom reading based on the article in Ready for C1 Advanced, Unit 5.

HOW TO READ

After reading, be ready to discuss the Think questions under the sections. Your teacher may choose any of them.

SECTION A

Ten years apart

The two brothers have been estranged for a decade. They no longer keep in touch directly; instead, news about one another filters through relatives. What may once have been an ordinary disagreement has gradually fractured their relationship.

The writer believes reconciliation is now extremely unlikely. He finds the situation distressing, yet argues that adult siblings falling out is far from exceptional. Resentment can accumulate over years until emotional distance begins to feel normal.

ThinkWhat seems to keep the conflict alive: one major event, or an accumulation of smaller experiences?
SECTION B

Where rivalry begins

Sibling rivalry can emerge remarkably early. A first-born child may interpret the arrival of a younger sibling as a loss of attention or status, while perceived injustice in parental treatment can create lasting resentment.

Comparison may put a strain on a relationship even when parents have no intention of encouraging competition. If siblings pursue the same kind of recognition or professional success, rivalry can intensify and apparently minor differences may become a source of long-term friction.

ThinkWhich is more damaging in the long term: competition itself or the feeling of being treated unfairly?
SECTION C

Different people, same family

Some siblings eventually learn to manage conflict because they do not want hostility to become an acceptable family pattern. Others remain poles apart in temperament and struggle to find common ground.

The writer remembers being expected to keep an eye on his younger brother. Rather than bringing them closer, this responsibility bred resentment. The roles assigned to them in childhood became difficult to shake off, and neither brother seemed able to bridge the gap as they grew older.

ThinkHow can family roles shape a relationship even when nobody deliberately chooses those roles?
SECTION D

Patterns we pass on

Leaving home gave the brothers some breathing space, but physical distance did not resolve the underlying tension. Their encounters became increasingly strained until, eventually, they turned their backs on each other.

Now a parent himself, the writer worries about passing dysfunctional behavioural patterns down to the next generation. He would be distraught if his daughters repeated the same pattern. The question is no longer simply what families inherit, but which patterns people can consciously interrupt.

ThinkWhich family patterns can people consciously choose to change?

PAIR DISCUSSION · 4–5 MIN

Do we inherit family relationships?

First, your teacher may ask one or more of the Think questions from sections A–D. Then choose one claim and agree, disagree or qualify it. Support your view with evidence from the reading and use at least two phrases from your green language bank.

“Childhood roles follow siblings into adulthood.”
“Distance solves conflict better than confrontation.”
“We can choose which family patterns to pass on.”
