Sibling rivalry:
when brothers fall out

An adapted classroom reading based on the article in Ready for C1 Advanced, Unit 5.

After reading, be ready to discuss the Think questions under the sections. Your teacher may choose any of them for pair or whole-class discussion.

SECTION A

Ten years apart

Two brothers have not spoken directly for a decade. During that time, both of their lives have changed, but news about each other has travelled through relatives rather than through conversation.

The writer believes reconciliation is now extremely unlikely. He regrets this, but also argues that serious conflict between adult siblings is far from rare. Resentment can build up slowly until distance begins to feel normal.

ThinkWhat seems to keep the conflict alive: one major event, or an accumulation of smaller experiences?
SECTION B

Where rivalry begins

Research on sibling relationships suggests that tension may begin surprisingly early. An older child can experience the arrival of a younger sibling as a loss of attention, status or fairness.

Differences in parental treatment can deepen those feelings, even when parents do not intend to create competition. In one family, two brothers also shared the same professional ambition, which made comparison difficult to escape.

ThinkWhich is more damaging in the long term: competition itself or the feeling of being treated unfairly?
SECTION C

Different people, same family

Some siblings eventually learn to avoid open conflict because they do not want their own children to see hostility as an acceptable family pattern.

The writer's experience was different. He and his brother had very different personalities and struggled to find common ground. As the older child, he was often expected to look after his younger brother and came to resent that responsibility rather than see it as closeness.

ThinkHow can family roles shape a relationship even when nobody deliberately chooses those roles?
SECTION D

Patterns we pass on

Leaving home finally gave the brothers some breathing space, but distance did not repair the relationship. Their occasional meetings became increasingly tense and eventually the relationship broke down completely.

Now a parent himself, the writer worries less about repeating particular arguments than about passing a dysfunctional pattern on to the next generation. He wants his daughters to inherit something different.

ThinkWhich family patterns can people consciously choose to change?

PAIR DISCUSSION · 4–5 MIN

Do we inherit family relationships?

First, your teacher may ask one or more of the Think questions from sections A–D. Then choose one claim below and agree, disagree or qualify it using evidence from the reading and at least two green phrases.

“Childhood roles follow siblings into adulthood.”
“Distance solves conflict better than confrontation.”
“We can choose which family patterns to pass on.”
